## Overview

The full equations of motion for a rotating, stratified fluid have a dozen
terms. At any *given* scale, most of them are negligible — and this
chapter's entire content is the mechanical procedure for finding out which:
nondimensionalize the Boussinesq equations by a length $L$, velocity $U$,
and latitude, and the dimensionless coefficient in front of each term
answers "does this term matter here?" directly. Every reduced equation set
used later in the book (geostrophic balance, quasi-geostrophy, internal
waves, Boussinesq convection) is what survives this procedure at that
chapter's particular scale.

## The model

Notebook: `notebooks/ch02_governing-equations.py` → exported to
`/nb/ch02_governing-equations/`. Governing equations use the symbols in
[notation]({{< relref "notation.md" >}}).

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0,\qquad
  \frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}+f\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf u=-\nabla\phi+b\hat{\mathbf z}+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf u,
  \qquad \frac{Db}{Dt}=\kappa\nabla^2 b.$$

`gfdlib.scaling` computes $Ro,\ Ek,\ Fr,\ Re,\ Bu$ and the deformation
radius $L_R$ directly from physical inputs ($U,L,H,N,\nu$, latitude) — a
pure calculator, no discretization or time-stepping anywhere.

## Both fluids

- **Atmosphere:** midlatitude synoptic weather sits at $Ro\sim0.1$
  (geostrophically balanced); a single convective thunderstorm cell, at
  $L\sim10\,$km, sits at $Ro\gtrsim1$ — genuinely different dynamical
  regimes governed by the identical Boussinesq equations, distinguished
  only by scale.
- **Ocean:** mesoscale eddies sit close to $Bu\sim1$ ($L\sim L_R$), which
  is exactly why they are the scale most efficiently generated by
  baroclinic instability (Ch. 16) — the regime map in Ch. 4 makes this
  connection explicit.

## Exercises

1. *(analytic)* Starting from the dimensional horizontal momentum equation,
   substitute $x=L\hat x,\ u=U\hat u,\ t=(L/U)\hat t,\ \phi=f_0UL\hat\phi$
   and confirm you recover
   $Ro\,D\hat{\mathbf u}/D\hat t+\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf u}=-\nabla\hat\phi+\cdots$
   with $Ro=U/(f_0L)$ appearing exactly as the coefficient of the
   acceleration term.
2. *(computational)* Modify `ch02_governing-equations.py` to add a sixth
   preset of your choosing (e.g. a hurricane, $U\sim50\,$m/s,
   $L\sim200\,$km) and read off which terms the calculator says survive.
3. *(exploratory)* Using only the sliders, find a combination of
   $(U,L,H,N,\nu,\text{lat})$ where **all five** numbers are simultaneously
   $O(1)$ — i.e. no single simplification applies. Is this an easy region
   of parameter space to find, or a narrow one? What does that suggest
   about how often "the fully general equations, no simplification" is
   actually the right tool in GFD?
